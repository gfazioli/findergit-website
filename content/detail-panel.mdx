# Detail Panel

When you select a repository — in the [Repository List](/docs/repository-list) or the file browser — the detail panel is one page. A **header** stays at the top (name, path, last commit, the branch menu and ahead/behind), the **page** scrolls under it, and a **docked action bar** is pinned at the bottom. There is no tab strip: the page is always the repository's Overview, and its **cards** are the way into everything else.

![Detail panel showing the Overview page — the repository's pulse, a card for every section, and a year of activity, with Fetch/Pull/Push/Refresh docked at the bottom](/screenshot-detail-overview.png)

The page holds, from the top:

- **Notes** — the linked worktrees of the repository and, when it is a fork, the [fork banner](/docs/github-integration#fork-awareness)
- **The hero** — how alive the repository is, its age and latest tag, its description, **Open on GitHub** (the label follows the remote's host), the **Export** menu and the commits of the last 30 days as a sparkline
- **The cards** — one per section, each with a figure and a line of detail
- **The charts** — the year of activity, rhythm, codebase, latest commits and releases, and contributors

The cards, in the order they appear:

- **Changes** — working-tree changes, the diff, the commit form (including the AI commit button) and the stashes
- **Commits** — the repository's history, loaded as you scroll
- **Branches** — local and remote branches with one-click checkout, and the linked worktrees
- **Tags** — every tag beside the release cut from it, and what only exists on one side
- **Issues** — open issues on the repo's forge
- **Pull Requests** — open pull requests (merge requests on GitLab) on the repo's forge
- **Stars** — the star history, on GitHub
- **Maintenance** — disk-space breakdown and cleanup (see [Repo Maintenance](/docs/repo-maintenance))
- **Trust** — the repo's auto-run surface (see [Repo Trust](/docs/repo-trust))

A repository on GitHub has all nine. On another forge there are eight (no Stars), and with no remote there are six (no Issues, Pull Requests or Stars). The cards read in order of what they are about: what you are doing right now, then this repository's refs, then what the forge says about it, then looking after the place. Tags sits beside Branches because a tag is a ref and the two answer the same kind of question.

## Opening a card

Click a card to open its section. The card grows into a panel under its row, joined to the row as one shape in the card's colour; the other cards step back and the charts give way. Click the open card, or the **×** at the panel's top right, to close it and bring the charts back. One card is open at a time.

The open card is remembered: select another repository and the same card is open there, when that repository has it, and so is the next launch. A repository that lacks the card (Issues on one with no remote) does not lose your choice — the next repository that has it opens it again.

### Scrolling inside the panel

The panel stops at the bottom edge of the window. A long list — commits, a hundred tags, an expanded issue — scrolls inside it while the header, the hero and the cards stay where they are. The controls a section pins stay at the top of the panel as the list moves under them: **New Issue**, **Open on…** and **Refresh** in Issues and Pull Requests, and the tag bar in Tags.

### Buttons and hover

Everything that acts in a section is a button with an icon, tinted by what it does, and with a hover state: Stage, Unstage, Discard, the stash actions, the tag bar's **Check remote**, **New tag**, **Open on GitHub** and **Refresh**, and the actions on a row. Rows highlight under the pointer in the card's colour. Forge labels are solid chips, with text chosen to read on them.

## Pull to refresh

On macOS 27, pull down from the top of the detail panel to refresh its cards and the open section. This works with short lists, empty results and load errors too. The spinner lets go after at most a couple of seconds while slower work continues.

- The **cards** re-read their figures: the repository's history and tags, forge metadata and counts, fork information and worktrees. With no card open, the charts are drawn again.
- **Changes**, **Commits** and **Branches** re-read local status, history or branch lists.
- **Issues** and **Pull Requests** bypass the cache and refresh their counts and any expanded thread.
- **Tags** reloads local tags and forge releases, and repeats the remote-tag check if it was already loaded.
- **Maintenance** re-runs the disk-space report; expensive history analyses remain opt-in.
- **Trust** re-scans the selected repository without changing its trust setting.

Pulling never performs Fetch, Pull, Push or cleanup. The Issues, Pull Requests and Tags controls stay pinned at the top of their panel. On older macOS versions, use the existing refresh buttons.

## Docked action bar

The bottom edge of the panel carries:

- A compact **branch summary** — `branch · upstream · ↑N ↓M` (and stash count when non-zero), ending with **"Last fetched Xm ago"** so the background auto-fetch has visible evidence it's keeping the repo current
- The five repo-global actions: **Fetch · Pull · Push · Stash… · Refresh** — on a narrow panel, the ones that don't fit fold into a **⋯** menu (see [Responsive compact mode](/docs/git-actions#responsive-compact-mode))

Because the bar is docked outside the scroll area, the actions stay reachable whichever card is open — you don't have to leave Commits to push, or leave Issues to pull.

## Overview

The Overview is the page itself: a dashboard of the selected repository, and a map of the sections the cards open. Most of it is read from the repository itself, so it fills in for a local-only repo too; a remote on GitHub, Codeberg, GitLab or Bitbucket adds the description, the stars, and the open issues and pull requests (see [GitHub Integration](/docs/github-integration#what-populates-from-github) for what each forge can fill in).

- **The repository at a glance** — how alive it is (*Active*, *Steady*, *Quiet* or *Dormant*, from its newest commit), when its history starts, its latest tag and how many commits sit on top of it, and whether one person wrote it. The headline is the forge's description, or the opening sentence of the README when there isn't one, with the topics under it and an **Open on GitHub** button (the label follows the remote's host). Beside it, the commits of the last 30 days over a 26-week sparkline.
- **A card for every section** — each shows a figure and a line of detail, in the colour and icon of its section:
  - **Changes** — the number of changed files; the line says *Working tree clean*, *Nothing staged yet*, *Partly staged*, *All staged, ready to commit* or the number of conflicts, and adds the stash count when there are stashes. It turns red on conflicts.
  - **Commits** — the number of commits; the line is the distance from the upstream, like `↑0 ↓1 · origin/main`, or *In step with* the upstream, *No upstream*, *Detached HEAD* or *No commits yet*. It takes the colour of what asks for something: orange when behind, purple when only ahead.
  - **Branches** — the number of local branches; the line gives the current branch and the number of linked worktrees (or, with none, how many branches exist only on remotes). In a linked worktree it says which repository it belongs to.
  - **Tags** — the number of tags; the line is the latest tag, or *No tags yet*.
  - **Issues** and **Pull Requests** — the open count on the forge; the line names the forge, or says *None open*, or that the forge does not track it.
  - **Stars** — the star count; the line gives forks and watchers.
  - **Maintenance** — what the repository weighs on disk; the line gives its number of tracked files.
  - **Trust** — the number of findings, in the colour of the worst: red for critical findings, orange when the auto-run surface changed, yellow for hooks to review, green when there is nothing to review.
- **Activity** — the last year as a calendar, one square per day, or as a line through its days, with the current and the longest streak. The switch beside the streaks picks one for every repository, and an exported picture draws the same. Hover a day in the calendar to read how many commits it holds.
- **Rhythm** — the same commits by weekday and hour, with the busiest day and the peak hour.
- **Codebase** — the languages by size, and whether the repository carries a README, a license, a `.gitignore`, a contributing guide, a security policy and CI.
- **Latest commits** and **Releases** — the five newest commits and the five newest tags as timelines, with how often a release ships and how many commits are still unreleased. A row opens the Commits or Tags card.
- **Contributors** — who wrote it, with how much, when more than one person did. A solo project is named at the top instead.

What the old stash, upstream and worktree counts showed is now carried in those lines of detail: stashes in Changes, the upstream in Commits, worktrees in Branches.

The **Stars** history lives in the Stars card's section: on a GitHub remote, the repository's stars since it was created — the running total, the stars of the last 30 days, of the last year once it has one, and of the best week. A switch beside them draws the history as steps, a smooth line, or bars of each month's new stars, and **Open on GitHub** takes you to the people who starred it.

The cards arrive one after another and the charts draw themselves when you select the repository, and again when you refresh. Nothing moves on its own after that, and with Reduce Motion turned on everything is simply there.

The numbers follow the repository as it changes: a commit, a pull, a checkout or a new tag updates them, and the calendar moves to the next day at midnight.

### Saving it as an image or a PDF

**Export**, beside **Open on GitHub**, saves the Overview as a PNG or a PDF, or copies it as an image to paste anywhere. The picture is the whole dashboard at full height and two columns wide, whatever the size of the window, with the repository's name, its branch and the date on top. It follows the app's light or dark appearance, at your display's resolution, and it is drawn from what the Overview already shows, so the numbers are the ones on screen.

On a GitHub repository the same menu holds **Put It in Your README with glancecard…**, which opens the glancecard window with that repository added, to show a card of it that redraws itself every day (see [Profile Card](/docs/profile-card#on-your-github-profile)).

## Changes

This is where the working-tree affordances live. The Changes card opens:

- The **changes list** (staged and unstaged paths, with status markers)
- An inline **diff viewer** for the selected change (see [Diff Viewer](/docs/diff-viewer))
- The **commit form** with the AI Commit button (see [AI Commit Messages](/docs/ai-commit-messages))

![The Changes card open under the hero and cards — changes list, inline diff, and the commit form](/screenshot-detail-unstage.png)

### Discard changes

Every unstaged row also carries a red **Discard**, and the section header a **Discard All** — the complement to Stage/Unstage when you want changes *gone* rather than staged:

- **Modified or deleted files** are reverted to the last staged/committed version.
- **Untracked files** are moved to the **Trash** — recoverable from Finder with "Put Back" — never deleted outright.

Both run behind a confirmation that states exactly what will happen, including the file count for Discard All. Discarding is destructive by nature; the Trash routing for untracked files is the safety net.

![Discard All in the Changes section with four unstaged changes, asking for confirmation before anything is reverted or moved to the Trash](/screenshot-discard.png)

### Stashed changes

Set the working tree aside without committing it. **Stash…** sits in the action bar with Fetch, Pull and Push, because it acts on the whole repository the way they do — staged work included. It also has to be reachable when nothing is unstaged, which is exactly the moment after Stage All when setting everything aside is what you want. While it runs the button says so, and after a few seconds it turns into Stop.

It asks for a message and takes an empty one: leave it blank and git names the stash after your last commit. Untracked files go too, so the tree is genuinely clean afterwards rather than half-stashed. On a clean tree it says **"Nothing to stash"** instead of claiming it saved something — the direction that matters, since the other way round leaves you believing your work is tucked away.

Existing stashes appear underneath the commit form as **Stashed changes (N)**, collapsed until you open it. Each row carries the message, the branch it was made on, and how long ago — and a grey **Automatic** badge when the text is git's rather than yours, so a subject you never typed doesn't read as a description you chose.

Three actions per row:

- **Pop** — apply the changes and remove the stash
- **Apply** — apply the changes and keep the stash
- **Drop** — delete it without applying, behind a confirmation that says plainly that the changes are not applied first

If a pop hits a conflict, git writes the conflict into your files and **keeps the stash** — nothing is lost, and the entry stays on the list so you can try again after resolving. The panel refreshes to show the conflicted files, and the failure stays on screen rather than flickering past.

![The Changes section with the stash list open — two entries, one carrying a typed message and one badged Automatic, each showing its branch and age with Pop, Apply and Drop on the right](/screenshot-stash.png)

## Commits

The Commits card opens the repository's history from local `git log`. It starts with a page of commits and loads more when you reach the end of the list, so a long history never has to be read in one go. Each row shows:

- The author's avatar on the left — from their GitHub account when the commit uses a GitHub noreply email, from Gravatar otherwise, or a colored monogram of their initials when neither is available
- The commit subject (up to two lines)
- Tag and branch badges when a ref points at the commit — **tags in gold**, branches and remote-tracking refs (`origin/main`, …) in grey, the current branch in the accent color
- The short SHA, author, and a relative date
- A copy button on the right that copies the full SHA to the clipboard — the icon flips to a green ✓ for ~1 second so the action has visual feedback

When the branch is **behind its upstream**, the commits a pull would bring in ride at the top in a distinct **"Incoming (N) · to pull"** card — each row marked with a ↓ — above your local history. So you see not just *that* you're behind, but *what* you're behind on. They're the commits from `git log HEAD..@{upstream}`, already on disk from the last fetch, so listing them costs nothing on the network. Pull, and the card folds away as they merge into the history below. Repos with no upstream — or already up to date — simply don't show it.

![The Commits card open, with an "Incoming (3) · to pull" card at the top — three behind-upstream commits marked with a ↓ and origin/main badges, set off above the local history, and "Last fetched now" in the docked action bar](/screenshot-commits-incoming.png)

### What a commit changed

Every commit row opens like a folder. Click the row — or the chevron on its left — and the files that commit touched unfold underneath it, with a **"N files · +additions −deletions"** summary on top and, per file, git's own status letter (**A**dded, **M**odified, **D**eleted, **R**enamed), the path, and that file's own line counts. A renamed file carries its old name underneath. Binary files say **binary** rather than pretending to a line count.

Click a file in that list and its diff for that commit opens in a window of its own — the same viewer the working-tree diff uses, scoped to what this one commit did to this one file. The window can be moved and resized, so you can read it beside the list; there is one window per file, and asking for the same file again brings its window forward. See [the commit diff window](/docs/diff-viewer#a-commits-diff-in-its-own-window). That includes **deleted** files: what a commit removed is often the part most worth reading, and the whole deletion is there. Only binary files have no diff to show. It is read-only: nothing here stages, discards or reverts.

The list is fetched only when you open a row, so the section still costs one `git log` per page no matter how much history you scroll. Merge commits list what they brought in against their first parent, rather than the empty list `git show` gives them by default.

![The Commits card with the newest commit opened onto its files — a "2 files +2" summary, then each path with git's status letter and its own line counts, tied to the commit above by a hairline](/screenshot-commit-files.png)

Hover any row to highlight it. Avatars and badges can each be turned off in [Settings → Appearance](/docs/settings#commits).

![The Commits card — each row leads with the author's avatar on the left; commits pointed at by a ref show badges above the subject (gold tag, accent current branch, grey origin/master and origin/HEAD), followed by subject, short SHA, author, relative date, and a copy button](/screenshot-detail-commits.png)

## Branches

Switch branches without leaving the app — no more discovering mid-task that you were on the wrong one. Three ways in:

- **The branch chip** in the panel header shows the current branch; click it for a dropdown of the repo's local branches and switch with one click.
- **The Branches card** opens the full view. The **checked-out branch is pinned at the top** in its own card, with its upstream and its ↑ahead ↓behind counts beside it, so the one branch you are actually on never scrolls away. Under it, **Local** and **Remote** are separate bounded groups, each with a count, each row separated and highlighted under the pointer. Every row is a target: click anywhere on it, or use its **Checkout** button. Checking out a remote branch creates a local branch tracking exactly that remote — unambiguous even when several remotes carry the same branch name.
- **Right-click a repo** in the file browser → **Switch Branch** for the same quick switch from the list.

Every row also carries a **context menu** for the things that are not a switch — rename, merge, rebase, delete — and **New branch…** sits at the top of the dropdown. See [Managing branches](/docs/git-actions#managing-branches).

After a checkout the file tree, status badge, and branch chip all update automatically. If Git refuses the switch (typically uncommitted changes that would be overwritten), the error appears in the action-bar feedback — nothing is forced.

![The Branches card — Local and Remote sections with per-row Checkout, the current branch marked, and the branch chip in the header](/screenshot-branches.png)

### Worktrees

Under Local and Remote, the same section lists the repository's **linked worktrees** — the folders `git worktree add` creates, each holding a second branch checked out on its own. They live here because a worktree *is* a branch checked out somewhere else, and because this is where you end up when Git refuses to delete a branch one of them is holding.

Each row shows the folder, where it is, and the branch it carries — or `detached` when it is not on one. A padlock marks a worktree you have locked, and a **missing** badge marks one whose folder is gone. The row menu offers **Add to Root Folders** (start working in it here), **Reveal in Finder**, and **Remove…**.

**Add** creates one. Give it a branch — new or existing — and the folder is created **beside** this repository, named `<repo>--<branch>`: the layout `git worktree list` expects, and the one that keeps `git status` clean, since a worktree nested inside its own repository shows up as untracked files.

**Prune** appears only when there is something to clear: a worktree whose folder you deleted by hand stays registered, and that registration is what keeps `git branch -D` failing on its branch.

Removal is never forced. Git refuses to remove a worktree holding uncommitted changes, and FinderGit shows you that refusal rather than overriding it — the branch is always kept, only the folder goes.

## Issues and Pull Requests

The Issues and Pull Requests cards open the forge's lists. The controls stay pinned at the top of the panel as you scroll through the list or an expanded thread. **New Issue** / **New Pull Request**, **Open on…** and **Refresh** remain within reach, as the tag controls already do in Tags.

Each card carries its open count as its figure — so a repository says what is waiting on it before you open either card. The number is the forge's own count of open items — the same one behind the file browser's Issues and PRs columns, read from the same place, so it costs nothing extra for a repository already on screen. A card with nothing open says **None open** (the column still prints its `0`: a column with a heading needs a cell). A forge that has no such counter shows no figure — Bitbucket retired its issue tracker, so its Issues card stays bare rather than claiming zero.

Both sections share the same row shape:

- A GitHub-style state icon (filled circle for issues — green for open, purple for closed; pull-request arrows for PRs — green for open, grey for draft, purple for merged, red for closed)
- The `#number`
- The title (up to two lines)
- **When it was opened and by whom** — a quiet line under the title, so a row says its age without being unfolded. A pull request adds the branch pair it would merge, `head → base`, with a long head truncated in the middle because the informative half of a branch name is its tail
- Coloured label chips
- The author's avatar on the right

### Reading a thread without leaving the app

Click anywhere on a row and it unfolds in place: the **description rendered as Markdown** — headings, lists, links, inline and fenced code — under the author and when they opened it, followed by the **comment thread**. A long description folds after the first stretch with a **Show more** link, so one verbose issue cannot push the rest of the list off screen. One row is open at a time.

**The HTML a forge allows in Markdown is drawn, not printed.** A collapsed section (`<details>`) is a row you click to open, the way it is on the forge's page, so a bot's long review comment is a few titles rather than a wall; bold, links, line breaks, lists, tables and callouts (`> [!NOTE]`) read as what they are. Code is left exactly as written, and so is anything that only looks like a tag — `Array<String>` stays `Array<String>`.

On a **pull request** the preview shows the whole discussion, not just the conversation: the body a reviewer submitted with their verdict, and the notes pinned to lines of a file, each labelled for what it is. A pull request reviewed entirely inline — which is how most of them are — would otherwise look as though nobody had said anything.

The description costs nothing extra — every forge already returns it with the list. The comments are fetched only when you open a row, capped at the first 30, and cached for a minute like everything else in these sections. That is the same on GitHub, Codeberg and Bitbucket; GitLab shows comments only to a signed-in user, so its preview says so instead (see [GitLab](/docs/github-integration#gitlab)).

**Images in a description are not downloaded.** An issue body is written by anyone who can open an issue, so every image URL in it is chosen by someone else — and fetching one would tell them your IP address and when you read their issue, or reach a service only your Mac can see. FinderGit shows a placeholder and leaves the fetching to your browser, which is what the **Open in browser** button is for.

At the top of the preview, **Copy link** and **Open in browser** are there for when you do want github.com — a permalink to paste into a message, or the parts FinderGit does not render yet; under a discussion they are repeated, where you are when you have read it. You do not have to open a thread for its link: the **link button** at the right of each row copies it, and a **right-click** on the row copies the link, a Markdown link (`[#12 Title](…)`), the title, the number or, on a pull request, the branch name. A link in a thread opens only if it is a web or mail address.

![An issue opened in place — labels, the author and when they filed it, then the description rendered as Markdown with headings, inline code and a fenced block, folded behind Show more, with Copy link and Open in browser above it](/screenshot-issue-preview.png)

Each section's header has a **New Issue / New Pull Request** button (opens the corresponding "new" page), an **Open on GitHub** button (opens the full Issues / Pull Requests list page — the label follows the remote's host), and a **Refresh** button that bypasses the 60-second cache when you want fresh data.

On GitHub repos both sections need either a working `gh` CLI (see [GitHub Integration](/docs/github-integration)) or a Personal Access Token in Settings. On Codeberg and GitLab they need nothing — both APIs answer anonymously. On Bitbucket only Pull Requests apply: its issue tracker was retired, and the Issues card says so. A repo with no remote has no Issues or Pull Requests cards at all.

![The Issues card open — the cards above show the open counts of Issues and Pull Requests; below, each row carries a green open-issue icon, number, title, labels and author avatar, with New Issue, Open on GitHub, and Refresh in the header](/screenshot-detail-issues.png)

## Tags

![The Tags card — tags on the left with their presence, releases on the right, a filter and the tag count in the header](/screenshot-detail-release.png)

Tags and releases side by side, because the states worth seeing are the ones where they disagree: a tag nobody cut a release from, a release whose tag is not in your clone, a tag that exists here and not on the remote.

**One row is one version.** The tag is on the left, the release cut from it on the right. They are cells of the same row rather than two lists, so they cannot drift apart as tag messages wrap — which is exactly what happens on a repository whose tag messages run to a paragraph.

A tag row shows whether it is **annotated** (a filled tag icon — its own object, carrying a message, a tagger and a date) or **lightweight** (an outline icon — just a name pointing at a commit). Releases are cut from annotated tags. The row ends with the date, the short commit id, a button to open the tag on its forge, and a ⋯ menu.

The line under the name is **not always the same sentence**, and which one it is matters. An annotated tag shows its own message. A lightweight tag has none, so it shows the subject of the **commit** it points at, behind a small commit mark and in a lighter grey. They were written by different people at different times, and a commit subject printed under the heading "tag message" is a caption that lies — so the row labels which one you are reading rather than quietly filling the gap.

### Where each tag actually is

`git tag` lists what is in **your clone**, which is not the same set as what is on `origin` — and the difference is invisible in every tool that shows you one list. A tag you made and never pushed looks identical to one everybody has. A tag a colleague pushed this morning does not appear at all until you fetch.

So every row is in one of three states, and the two that need you carry a badge:

- **local only** — it exists here and not on origin. This is the one Push is for
- **not fetched** — origin has it and you do not. There is no local tag to delete, and **Fetch** on the row brings it down
- no badge — the name is on both once **Check remote** has answered (before that, nothing is known either way): usually nothing to do, and deleting locally leaves origin's copy

**Check remote** is what asks `origin`, and it is a separate button because it is the one thing here that touches the network — the tag list itself is local git and answers instantly. Until it has answered, no row claims either way: FinderGit will not tell you a tag is unpushed on the strength of a lookup it never made.

When the answer comes back with more than one tag you are missing, the header offers **Fetch N tags** to bring all of them down at once; a single one is fetched from its row. And each action is offered only where it can do something: Push appears on local-only rows, Fetch on not-fetched ones, and neither is drawn on a row where it would be meaningless.

### Finding one tag among hundreds

A long-lived repository can carry hundreds of tags — the count in the header is the honest number, and on a repository measured while building this it was over seven hundred. The **filter** field matches the tag name and **exactly what the row's second line says** — an annotated tag's message, or the commit subject a lightweight tag shows instead. So typing `rc` finds the release candidates, a word from a tag message finds the tag carrying it, and a word from a merge commit finds the lightweight tag sitting on it. The header says how many of the total are shown.

Both halves of that are deliberate. A filter that hides a row whose visible text contains what you typed is the worst shape a search can have — the words are on screen and the box says there is nothing there. Matching text the row does *not* show is the same fault wearing a friendlier face, and it returns rows with no visible reason to be there.

### Date or version — and why there is a choice

Rows are ordered **newest first, in one sequence**, whichever side supplies the date: a tag in your clone is dated by the tag itself, one you have not fetched by the release cut from it. So a tag pushed last week that you have never fetched sits at the top where it belongs, rather than in a block of its own after everything else.

That is the default, and on most repositories it is all you need. But "when was this tag made" is **two different questions** depending on the kind, and nothing on screen used to say so:

- an **annotated** tag is an object with a creation date, and that is the date you see;
- a **lightweight** tag is only a name. Git records nothing about when it was made — there is no such field — so the date shown is the **commit's**.

Which means a lightweight tag put on an older commit sorts below tags that look newer, and the list is correct while reading as though it were broken. On a real repository `v1.0.2` sat at the very bottom of two hundred rows, under `v1.0.1`, because the commit it names predates the `v1.0.1` tag object by six days.

So the header carries a **Date / Version** picker. Version order reads the names and nothing else, which also fixes the ordering that pure alphabetical gets backwards: `v26.10` belongs above `v26.2`, and a release belongs **above its own release candidates** — `v24.6.3` over `v24.6.3-rc.0`, not under it. Tags that are not version-shaped at all, a `latest` or a `stable`, are kept together at the end rather than interleaved among the numbers.

Hovering the date tells you which of the two it is, so a surprising position always has a visible explanation.

### Acting on many tags at once

Click a tag to select it, ⇧-click to extend the range, ⌘-click to add or remove one. A bar appears above the list with **Fetch**, **Push** and a **Delete** menu, and nothing changes until you use it. Each action applies to the part of the selection it can touch, and says how many that is — Push over five tags of which two are already on origin acts on three, and says three.

![Three tags selected across the three presence states, on a list scrolled far enough that a row is passing under the pinned header — the bar offers Fetch 1, Push 1 and a Delete menu, each counting only the tags it can act on, and one of the three selected tags is scrolled out of sight above](/screenshot-detail-release-selection.png)

The three deletions live behind one menu rather than as buttons in a row: **Delete locally**, **Delete on the remote**, and **Delete everywhere**. The last one does both sides in one go, and it runs **the remote first** — deliberately. If the remote deletion fails, a protected tag or a permission denied, your local tag has not been touched and nothing is lost. The other order would destroy the only remaining reference to that commit and only then discover it could not reach origin. When a run does stop half way, the result says **which side** it stopped on, because the recovery differs: a tag still on origin needs the whole thing again, one already gone from origin only needs deleting here.

![The Delete menu over a mixed selection: Delete 2 tags locally, Delete 2 tags on the remote, and — below a separator — Delete 1 tag everywhere. Three different numbers from one selection of three, because each item counts only the tags it applies to](/screenshot-detail-release-delete-menu.png)

**The header and that bar stay put while the list scrolls.** On a repository with seven hundred tags you will often build a selection near the bottom, and chrome that scrolls away with the rows takes the actions for that selection with it.

The selection covers the tag, never the release beside it. **A tag and its release are two separate objects with an optional link**, in both directions: deleting a release leaves its tag standing, and a release can even be created for a tag that does not exist yet.

Deletions run one at a time and report **an outcome per tag**, not per run. Ten pushes where three fail will tell you which three and what Git said about each — "already exists" and "denied" are different problems with different fixes. The ones that did not go through stay selected, so retrying does not mean picking them again. **Stop** ends the run, and whatever had not been attempted is reported as exactly that rather than as a failure.

While a run is going, the bar shows how far it has got — how many tags are **finished** out of how many, and the name of the one Git is holding right now, which is what tells a run wedged on an unreachable ref from a run that is merely slow. Every tag in the run says the same thing on its own row: waiting, running, done, failed. Closing the card or switching to another repository does not lose any of it: the run belongs to the repository, and coming back shows where it got to.

When the run ends, the bar does not simply disappear — it becomes the **result**, which stays there to be read, and stays until you dismiss it whenever something did not go through. That matters most for the fastest case: deleting ten tags locally is over in about a tenth of a second, and rows vanishing is not by itself a report of what happened to them.

### What deleting a tag on the remote actually does

Worth knowing before you use it, because it is not what most people expect:

- Your **local** tag stays. Only the copy on `origin` goes.
- Anyone who has already fetched the tag keeps theirs.
- **If a release was cut from that tag, GitHub does not delete it — it turns it into a draft.** The release disappears from the public releases page and moves to an `untagged-…` URL.
- **Pushing the tag back does not undo that.** The release stays a draft until someone re-publishes it by hand.

FinderGit says which of these applies before you confirm, and it knows because the section already pairs each tag with its release. A tag with no release gets the shorter, calmer sentence.

### Right-click

Both halves of a row carry a context menu, and they are different menus, because a tag and a release are different objects.

On the **tag** you get the same actions as the ⋯ button — Fetch, Push, Delete Locally…, Delete on Remote…, each only where it applies — plus **Open Tag on GitHub** (or GitLab, Codeberg, Bitbucket), **Copy Tag Link**, **Copy Tag Name** and, when the tag is in your clone, **Copy Commit SHA**. The same page is one click away on the row itself.

Those three appear only when the forge can be expected to *have* that page. On a tag marked **local only** — which is a measured answer, `origin` was asked and did not have it — they are not drawn at all, because a link into a 404 is worse than no link.

Right-clicking a tag that is part of a **multi-selection** gives you the actions for the selection, each labelled with the number it will act on. The deletions for the one row under the pointer are *not* repeated below them: two sets of delete items in one menu is not a scope you can read at a glance. What stays under the tag's own name is Copy, which cannot be ambiguous. This is also what macOS already does — right-clicking inside a selection in Finder acts on the selection.

![Right-clicking a tag that is inside a multi-selection: the selection's three deletions first, each saying it acts on 3 selected tags, then a separator, the name of the row under the pointer, and the Copy actions for that one tag underneath](/screenshot-detail-release-context-menu.png)

On the **release** you get **Open Release in Browser**, **Copy Release Link** and **Copy Tag Name**. Nothing appears on a row that has no release.

### Releases

The right-hand column is read from the forge: the release title, **draft** and **pre-release** badges, an attachment count, when it was published, and a button to open it in a browser.

A badge appears only when the forge actually reported it. GitLab has no draft or pre-release concept at all, so nothing is drawn there rather than "published" being implied by a flag it never sent. **Bitbucket has no releases**, and the section says so instead of showing an empty list under a heading.

A release whose tag is not in your clone is **not** an orphan: it gets an ordinary row, marked "not fetched", because the tag does exist — just not here. What lands in the separate section at the top is the genuinely stranded kind, typically a release left as a draft after its tag was deleted.

**"No release" is only ever said when it can be proved.** If the list came back short, or a refresh is in flight, or it failed, the count carries a **+** and no row asserts an absence — because the sentence matters: deleting a tag on GitHub turns its release into a draft, and a wrong "nothing else is affected" is how you find that out afterwards.
